# Rectocele (posterior vaginal wall prolapse)

A rectocele is a bulge of the back wall of the vagina. The rectum sits right behind that wall, so when the wall weakens, the rectum pushes forward into the vaginal canal. It is a type of pelvic organ prolapse, and you'll also see it called posterior vaginal prolapse or posterior vaginal wall prolapse.

## What is a rectocele?

The thin layer of connective tissue between the vaginal skin and the rectum is sometimes called the rectovaginal septum. Anatomy studies have shown it isn't a single, distinct sheet of tissue, which is why some experts prefer the broader term posterior vaginal wall prolapse. When this tissue stretches or weakens, the rectum (and sometimes other pelvic organs) can bulge into the vagina.

Rectoceles are common. Depending on the group of women studied, posterior wall prolapse is found in roughly 18% to 40%, and small rectoceles turn up often in women who have no symptoms at all.

## Symptoms

How much a rectocele bothers you depends mostly on its size. A small one may cause no symptoms. A large one can cause significant ones.

The most telling symptom is needing to press on the vagina or perineum, or put a finger in the vagina, to finish a bowel movement. This is called splinting. Other bowel symptoms include:

* A feeling that you haven't emptied completely
* Straining to pass stool
* A bulge in the vagina that gets bigger during bowel movements

Many women also feel, or sometimes see, a bulge in the vagina. It can feel like sitting on a ball or an egg.

{: .important-title }
> What you see or feel is likely not your rectum...
>
> ...it's the skin of the vagina. The rectum is behind that skin, and it's rarely visible at the vaginal opening.

## Is a rectocele dangerous?

Rarely. A rectocele is usually not serious, so there's seldom a reason to rush into treatment. It can get worse over time, though, so it's worth having it checked and followed. With diet and lifestyle changes, physical therapy, and sometimes surgery, the chance that your symptoms improve or go away is high.

## Risk factors

Several things raise your risk of a rectocele:

* Pregnancy and childbirth. Most women with a rectocele have had at least one vaginal delivery, and the risk goes up with each one. A very long labor or a large baby can stretch and weaken the pelvic floor muscles and the walls of the rectum and vagina.
* Chronic constipation. Straining over and over puts pressure on the back wall of the vagina.
* Being overweight. Extra weight presses on the pelvic floor whether you're sitting or standing.
* Chronic cough. Each cough adds pressure, and over years that can weaken the posterior vaginal wall.
* Repetitive heavy lifting and straining.
* Menopause and aging. The pelvic floor muscles (the levator ani) and connective tissue tend to weaken over time.

## How a rectocele is diagnosed

Your doctor will ask about your symptoms, then examine you. This exam is different from a routine pelvic exam, where the doctor looks at the cervix for a Pap smear and checks the uterus and ovaries. A pelvic floor exam looks at the vaginal walls, the pelvic floor muscles, the nerves that supply them, and how all of it works together.

During the exam, you'll be asked to cough, strain, or bear down so your doctor can see where any prolapse is and how large it is. You'll also be asked to squeeze your pelvic floor muscles so their strength can be checked.

Some doctors order a special x-ray called defecography, which shows the pelvic muscles while you strain. Most women with a vaginal bulge don't need it before choosing a treatment. It's most useful when the symptoms and the exam don't match.

## Treatment options

Treatment depends on how large the rectocele is and how much it bothers you. If you have no symptoms, you usually don't need treatment. Most women start with the simpler options below and move to a pessary or surgery only if those don't help enough.

### Watchful waiting

Many rectoceles cause no symptoms and are found during a routine exam. In that case, the most common approach is watchful waiting (also called active monitoring or expectant management): you and your doctor keep an eye on it. Regular checkups help you decide together when, or whether, to act.

### Managing constipation

Constipation is common with a rectocele, and the two feed each other. Straining can make the prolapse worse, and the prolapse can make stool harder to pass. Getting your bowels working well is a central part of treatment.

* Eat a high-fiber diet: whole-grain cereals, fruits, vegetables, and beans.
* Drink six to eight cups of water a day.
* Don't strain. If you can't empty without straining, get up and try again later.
* Use a footstool on the toilet. Raising your feet into a squat changes the angle of the rectum and makes it easier for many women to have a bowel movement.

Sometimes even soft stool is hard to pass because the rectocele has changed the anatomy. A footstool and splinting (below) can help in that situation.

### Reducing strain on the pelvic floor

Anything that raises pressure in your abdomen pushes on the back wall of the vagina. To keep a rectocele from getting worse:

* Find out what's causing a chronic cough and treat it. Each cough strains the pelvic floor.
* Avoid heavy lifting, and use good technique when you can't avoid it.
* Keep a healthy weight.
* Don't smoke.

### Kegels and pelvic floor physical therapy

Kegel exercises strengthen the pelvic floor muscles that support the rectum, bladder, and uterus. To do one, squeeze as if you're holding in urine when you don't need to go. Done regularly, Kegels can help keep a rectocele from getting worse. A pelvic floor physical therapist can check that you're using the right muscles and build a program around your symptoms.

### Splinting

Splinting means pressing on the back wall of the vagina, or on the perineum, during a bowel movement. Holding the bulge back lets the rectum empty more easily. It helps most if you have a large bulge and trouble finishing bowel movements, and some women use it while they wait for surgery.

### Pessary

A pessary is a silicone device placed in the vagina to support the vaginal walls and, through them, the pelvic organs. It lifts the prolapse and holds it in place. Many women with a pessary have no further symptoms, even though the prolapse is still there. It's a good choice if you want to avoid surgery or if surgery isn't safe for you.

### Surgery

If the options above don't help enough, your doctor may recommend surgery.

Before surgery, you'll go over your overall health, age, medical history, plans for future pregnancy, the details of your prolapse, and whether you want to stay sexually active. That conversation helps your surgeon choose the operation that fits you.

Rectocele repair is most often done through the vagina. The procedure is called a posterior colporrhaphy, or posterior vaginal repair. The goal is to reduce the bulge and improve your symptoms. Most repairs use your own tissue and dissolvable stitches, which is called a native tissue repair. Adding synthetic mesh or a biologic graft to a posterior repair has not been shown to improve results and carries added risks, so it isn't routinely recommended. When prolapse involves more than one part of the vagina, a surgeon may use an abdominal or laparoscopic approach, sometimes combined with other repairs.

Most women go home the same day or after one night. How long you stay depends mostly on the type of surgery and your other medical problems.

Most women's bowel symptoms improve after a posterior repair, but a small number develop new bowel symptoms afterward. That's one reason you and your surgeon should make this decision together.

## Can a rectocele be prevented?

You can't always prevent one, but the same habits that treat a rectocele lower your chances of developing a symptomatic one:

* Do Kegels regularly to strengthen the pelvic floor.
* Prevent constipation with fiber and water.
* Lift with good technique, or avoid heavy lifting.
* Keep a healthy weight.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you feel a vaginal bulge, need to press on the vagina to finish a bowel movement, or are still constipated after trying the steps above. A urogynecologist can examine you and talk through options, from bowel habits and a pessary to surgery, that fit your goals.
