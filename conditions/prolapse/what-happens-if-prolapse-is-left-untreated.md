# What happens if prolapse is left untreated?

Pelvic organ prolapse is not a medical emergency. Leaving it untreated is a valid choice for many women. Prolapse tends to change slowly over years, and in some women it never gets much worse. Knowing the natural course can help you decide when, or whether, to pursue treatment.

## The natural course of prolapse

Without treatment, prolapse generally follows one of three patterns:

- It stays stable, sometimes for years
- It gets slowly worse over months to years
- It goes up and down, often feeling worse after a long day on your feet or heavy activity

In one study, 78% of women with untreated prolapse had no change after 16 months. In postmenopausal women followed over time, about 1 in 5 who had prolapse on exam no longer had it a year later. Over two years, 10% to 20% of women move up a stage. Only about 11% of postmenopausal women had their prolapse drop 2 cm or more over three years.

Prolapse is not like cancer. It does not spread. The real question is whether it bothers you enough to want treatment.

## What can happen if prolapse gets worse

As prolapse progresses, you may notice:

- A stronger bulge feeling, which is the most common symptom
- Trouble emptying your bladder fully, sometimes needing to shift position or push the bulge back to urinate
- Trouble finishing a bowel movement, sometimes needing to press on the vagina or the skin in front of the anus to get stool out
- Discomfort with exercise, walking, or standing for long periods
- Awareness of the bulge during sex

Prolapse can also cause bladder symptoms you might not connect to it, such as urgency, frequent urination, and a slow or stop-and-start stream. In a large trial of women having prolapse surgery, fixing the prolapse alone cleared bothersome urgency and frequency symptoms in about 75% of women, and bothersome emptying symptoms in about 85%. If you have prolapse and bladder symptoms, the prolapse may be the cause.

## Complications of advanced prolapse

With very advanced, long-standing prolapse:

- Vaginal tissue that sits outside the body can become dry, thickened, or develop sores
- Trouble emptying the bladder can lead to repeated urinary tract infections
- A large prolapse can press on the ureters (the tubes that drain urine from your kidneys to your bladder). About 30% of women whose prolapse extends 1 cm or more past the vaginal opening have some kidney swelling on imaging

These problems are uncommon in women with mild or moderate prolapse. If your prolapse is large and you have trouble emptying your bladder, treatment can make sense even when the bulge itself doesn't bother you much.

## When to think about treatment

The right time for treatment is when prolapse affects your daily life. For most women there is no medical deadline. The decision depends on your symptoms and what matters to you.

I see women who have had prolapse for 20 years and are doing fine without treatment. I see others who want treatment right away. Both are reasonable. My job is to help you understand your options so you can choose what feels right.

If you do want treatment, there are good nonsurgical options. Pelvic floor physical therapy can reduce prolapse symptoms. A pessary (a removable support device placed in the vagina) can be fitted in most women, including many with advanced prolapse. About a third of women stop using a pessary in the first year, but those who keep going past that point usually stay with it. These are not just stepping stones to surgery. For many women, they are the treatment.
