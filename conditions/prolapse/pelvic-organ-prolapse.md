# Understanding Pelvic Organ Prolapse (POP)

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is a common condition in women where pelvic organs shift into spaces they wouldn't normally occupy. Think of it as a hernia of the pelvic floor. This page covers the types of prolapse, the symptoms, how I diagnose it, and your treatment options.

## Types of Pelvic Organ Prolapse

POP is a general term that covers several specific types, each named for the organ that drops:

| Type | What drops | Part of the vagina affected | Symptoms patients often notice |
|------|-----------|-----------------------------|--------------------------------|
| [Cystocele](/conditions/cystocele) | Bladder | Front (anterior) wall | Bulge or pressure, urine leakage, spraying when you urinate |
| Urethrocele | Urethra | Lower front wall, near the opening | Usually found along with a cystocele; urinary symptoms |
| [Rectocele](/conditions/rectocele) | Rectum | Back (posterior) wall | Trouble emptying the bowels, pressing on or near the vagina to finish a bowel movement |
| [Enterocele](/conditions/enterocele) | Small intestine | Top of the vagina, toward the back | Pressure at the top of the vagina, worse with standing or straining |
| [Uterine prolapse](/conditions/uterine-prolapse) | Uterus | Top (apex) | Heaviness or pulling, a bulge; in advanced cases the cervix shows outside the vagina |
| Cervical prolapse | Cervix | Top (apex) | Bulge or pressure, including after a hysterectomy that left the cervix in place |
| [Vaginal vault prolapse](/conditions/vaginal-prolapse) | Top of the vagina after a hysterectomy | Top (apex) | Fullness or pressure, a bulge at the opening |

{: .important-title }
> Multiple Types of Prolapse Often Occur Together
>
> Many patients have more than one type of pelvic organ prolapse at the same time. For instance:
> 
> - About 70% of patients with a cystocele (bladder prolapse) also have 'apical' prolapse involving the uterus, cervix, or vaginal cuff.
> - Having more than one type of prolapse can change which symptoms you notice and which treatments make sense.
> - That's why I examine each compartment before recommending a plan.

## Symptoms of Pelvic Organ Prolapse

POP isn't dangerous, but it can be very bothersome. Common symptoms include:

- Feelings of pelvic pressure, fullness, pulling, or tugging (especially with heavy lifting)
- Sensation of sitting on an egg or ball
- Visible or palpable bulge at the vaginal opening
- Difficulty with vaginal intercourse
- Urinary symptoms (frequency, urgency, or incontinence)
- Spraying during urination
- Difficulty with bowel movements
- Abnormal vaginal bleeding (Note: Always consult a doctor for abnormal bleeding)

## Diagnosis of Pelvic Organ Prolapse

We diagnose POP with a pelvic exam, which includes:

1. Internal and external examination
2. Use of a speculum
3. Asking the patient to bear down, push, or cough to make the prolapse more pronounced

I use the POP-Q (Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification) system to stage prolapse on a scale from 0 to 4, where 0 is no prolapse and 4 is complete prolapse.

The stage depends on where the lowest point of the prolapse sits relative to the hymen, the ring of tissue at the vaginal opening, while you bear down:

| POP-Q stage | Where the lowest point of the prolapse sits | In plain terms |
|-------------|---------------------------------------------|----------------|
| 0 | No descent | No prolapse |
| I | More than 1 cm above the hymen | Inside the vagina, above the opening |
| II | Between 1 cm above and 1 cm below the hymen | At the opening |
| III | More than 1 cm below the hymen, but not complete | Outside the opening |
| IV | Complete eversion, or within 2 cm of it | The whole vagina is turned outward |

The stage describes anatomy. I decide whether to treat based on how much the prolapse bothers you.

## Treatment Options for Pelvic Organ Prolapse

POP has several effective treatments, and the right one depends on your symptoms and your goals. At your consultation, we'll talk through each option so you can make an informed decision. Some women need surgery and some don't. Some start with conservative treatment and some go straight to surgery. It depends on your situation.

### Observation (Expectant Management)

If your prolapse isn't bothering you, it's reasonable to watch it over time. Prolapse often remains stable, and not every prolapse requires treatment.

### Home Exercises

Kegel exercises to strengthen pelvic floor muscles, often combined with core strengthening and postural changes, can help alleviate symptoms.

### Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy

A pelvic floor physical therapist gives you supervised, hands-on treatment that goes further than exercises on your own at home. Studies show this can improve symptoms in women with mild to moderate prolapse.

### Pessary

A pessary is a device worn inside the vagina to support the pelvic organs. It's a non-surgical option that can be managed either by the patient at home or by a healthcare provider in the office. Up to 92% of women can be successfully fitted with a pessary, and many find it makes them noticeably more comfortable day to day.

### Surgical Options

For women with bothersome symptoms who want a more definitive solution, surgery can be very effective. The best surgical approach depends on the type and severity of prolapse, your overall health, and your goals. Options include:

- **Vaginal Repair**
Surgery performed entirely through the vagina, using the patient's own tissues and sutures to restore normal vaginal support.

- **Robotic-Assisted Laparoscopic Sacrocolpopexy**
A minimally invasive approach using small abdominal incisions and robotic assistance to attach a lightweight mesh to the vagina and sacrum, providing durable support. Learn more about [robotic sacrocolpopexy](/treatments/prolapse/robotic-sacrocolpopexy).

- **Colpocleisis (Obliterative Repair)**
An option for women who do not desire future vaginal intercourse. This procedure has high success rates and shorter recovery times.

## Making a Decision About Treatment

The right treatment depends on several factors that are unique to you:

- The severity and type of prolapse
- How your symptoms affect your daily life
- Your overall health and medical history
- Your goals for treatment (e.g., preserving sexual function, avoiding surgery)
- Your lifestyle and occupation

### Decision Aids

One helpful tool is the "[Should I Have Surgery](https://www.healthwise.net/ohridecisionaid/Content/StdDocument.aspx?DOCHWID=av1031)?" decision aid, which walks through the condition, treatment options, and potential outcomes. It includes comparisons of surgical and non-surgical options, personal stories from other patients, and questions to help you think about what matters most to you.

Reviewing something like this before your consultation can help you come in with a clearer sense of your priorities, which makes our conversation more productive.

## Next Steps

If you have symptoms of pelvic organ prolapse, you don't have to live with them. The first step is an exam so we can see what's going on and decide what makes sense for you.

To get started, schedule an appointment for an evaluation and a treatment plan built around your goals.
