# Does Menopause Cause Urinary Incontinence?

Menopause doesn't directly cause incontinence, but the drop in estrogen can make bladder control worse. Doctors use the term "genitourinary syndrome of menopause" (GSM) for the vaginal, urinary, and sexual symptoms that come from low estrogen. Hot flashes tend to fade over time, but GSM usually gets worse without treatment.

As estrogen drops, the tissue lining the urethra, vagina, and bladder gets thinner and less stretchy. The urethra and the base of the bladder grow from the same tissue as the vagina before birth, so they all respond to estrogen.

## The estrogen connection

Estrogen helps keep pelvic tissue healthy. It keeps blood flowing to the lining of the urethra, and that cushion of blood vessels helps the urethra seal shut. When estrogen drops during perimenopause and menopause:

- The urethral lining thins, so it doesn't seal as tightly
- Vaginal and pelvic tissue loses stretch and strength
- Blood flow to the area goes down
- The bladder can get more sensitive, so you feel the urge to go more often

I sometimes compare estrogen to fertilizer for the pelvic floor tissue. When it's around, the tissue stays thick and resilient. When it drops, the tissue becomes more vulnerable, especially if there was already some weakness from childbirth or other causes.

## How it shows up

Women going through menopause may notice:

- New leaking that wasn't a problem before
- Mild leaking that used to be manageable getting worse
- More urgency and more trips to the bathroom, including at night
- Burning with urination
- Repeat bladder infections, since thinner tissue is easier for bacteria to take hold
- Vaginal dryness, irritation, or pain with sex alongside the bladder symptoms

## Treatment options

Menopause-related bladder changes respond well to treatment. What I recommend depends on what kind of leaking you have and what bothers you most, and we decide together. You don't have to work through the options in a fixed order.

- Vaginal estrogen, as a cream, ring, or tablet, treats the tissue directly. In studies it improved urge leaking and cut down on repeat bladder infections. The benefit for leaking with coughing or exercise is smaller and less certain. Vaginal estrogen and systemic hormone therapy (pills or patches) are not interchangeable. Vaginal estrogen improved incontinence in studies, while systemic estrogen made it slightly worse.
- Pelvic floor physical therapy works at any age. In one large trial, postmenopausal women did at least as well with it as younger women. My practice has pelvic floor therapists in the office. One small study found that adding vaginal estrogen to pelvic floor therapy helped stress leaking more than estrogen alone.
- For leaking with coughing, sneezing, or exercise (stress incontinence): pelvic floor therapy, a pessary, urethral bulking, or a sling.
- For urgency and urge leaking: bladder training, fluid and caffeine changes, medication, Botox in the bladder, or nerve stimulation.
- For mixed incontinence, I treat whichever type bothers you most first.

A lot of my menopausal patients are surprised by how much vaginal estrogen helps their bladder, especially the urgency and the infections. It's simple and low risk, and it treats the tissue change behind the symptoms. Most women never mention these symptoms to a doctor, and many doctors don't ask. If this sounds like you, bring it up at your next visit.
