# Can physical therapy help urinary incontinence?

Yes, and it's often the most effective first-line treatment. Pelvic floor physical therapy involves working with a specialized therapist who assesses your pelvic floor muscles and develops a targeted program to improve their strength, coordination, and endurance. For many women, it reduces or eliminates incontinence without medication or surgery.

## How it works

Kegel exercises are only a small part of pelvic floor physical therapy. Your muscles need strength, but they also need to relax and lengthen, coordinate with each other, and hold up over time. The best physical therapists work on all of that.

A specialized therapist will also look at the surrounding structures (hips, thighs, and core) because they all affect how your pelvic floor works. Many patients improve a lot with changes to breathing, posture, and bracing alone. Your therapist may use biofeedback to show you which muscles to engage, develop a personalized program for your specific patterns, and progress your exercises as you improve.

## What to expect

Your first visit typically lasts about an hour and includes a detailed discussion of your symptoms and goals, an external and internal assessment of your pelvic floor muscles, education about your anatomy, and initial exercises to start at home.

Follow-up sessions (usually weekly or biweekly) involve progress assessment, advancing your exercise program as muscles strengthen, behavioral strategies for managing urgency, and techniques for using your pelvic floor muscles during activities that trigger leaking.

## The evidence

Studies show that pelvic floor therapy roughly halves the number of incontinence episodes for many women, and about half of women with stress-predominant incontinence are satisfied with their results a year after starting. It also helps manage urgency and urge incontinence through muscle coordination and urgency-suppression techniques, and it carries very little risk.

Whether your leaking is stress-related (with coughing, sneezing, or exercise), urgency-related (a sudden, hard-to-defer urge), or a mix of both, pelvic floor therapy is a reasonable place to start, and it stays useful even if you later add medication or surgery.

## Dr. Stewart's perspective

"Pelvic floor physical therapy is one of the most underutilized treatments in medicine. It's my first recommendation for nearly every type of incontinence, and it's an investment in your pelvic floor regardless of what other treatments we pursue."
