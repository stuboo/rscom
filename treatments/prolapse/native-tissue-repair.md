# Native Tissue Repair for Prolapse

Native tissue repair is prolapse surgery that uses your own tissues to restore support to the pelvic organs. It is well established and works for many women who want long-term relief from prolapse symptoms. It is also the most common type of prolapse surgery in the United States.

## What is Native Tissue Repair?

Your surgeon uses your body's own tissues to reinforce and support the pelvic organs. The goal is to restore normal pelvic floor anatomy and function without synthetic materials.

## Types of Native Tissue Repairs

The type of repair depends on which part of the vagina has prolapsed and how severe it is:

1. **Anterior Repair (Cystocele Repair)**: Addresses prolapse of the anterior wall of the vagina and bladder
2. **Posterior Repair (Rectocele Repair)**: Corrects prolapse of the posterior wall of the vagina and the rectum
3. **Apical Repair**: Treats prolapse of the uterus or vaginal vault (in women who have had a hysterectomy)
   - May be performed with a hysterectomy if the uterus is still present
   - Often involves uterosacral ligament suspension or sacrospinous ligament fixation
4. **Perineorrhaphy**: Reinforces the perineal body between the vagina and rectum

Many women need a combination of these procedures to fix more than one area of prolapse. Restoring support to the top (apex) of the vagina is central to a durable repair, which is why an apical suspension is usually included even when the most obvious bulge is in the front or back wall.

## The Surgical Procedure

Native tissue repair is usually performed under general anesthesia. Most procedures are done through the vagina, but some can be performed laparoscopically. The steps depend on the type of prolapse, but generally include:

1. Making an incision in the vaginal wall (or small abdominal incisions for laparoscopic procedures)
2. Identifying and repairing any defects in the supportive tissue
3. Reinforcing the area with sutures to provide additional support
4. Closing the incision(s)

The procedure typically takes 1-3 hours, depending on the extent of repair needed.

## Recovery and Aftercare

Recovery varies from person to person, but in general:

- Hospital stay is usually 0-1 day
- Full recovery takes about 6-8 weeks
- Pelvic rest (no intercourse or tampon use) is recommended for 6-8 weeks

Dr. Stewart takes a common-sense approach to post-operative activity restrictions:

> "I don't give people lifting restrictions because the data shows that this does not improve surgical outcomes. Instead, I favor a common sense approach to recovery: if it hurts, don't do it."

You listen to your body and get back to normal activities as you feel ready.

You'll have follow-up appointments to check how you're healing.

Your doctor will give you specific recovery instructions, which may vary based on the exact procedure and your overall health.

## Success Rates and Longevity

Native tissue repair is effective for most women, and results depend on the type and severity of prolapse. Securing the top of the vagina with an apical suspension (uterosacral or sacrospinous ligament fixation) is the part of the repair most responsible for lasting support. The two most common apical procedures work about equally well.

Some loss of support can develop over the years, and it is more likely when prolapse was severe before surgery. Even so, most women stay satisfied and never need a second operation. Symptom relief and quality of life tend to hold up over time even when a follow-up exam shows some settling. Your doctor can talk with you about what to expect in your case and how it compares with other options, including a pessary or a mesh-based repair such as sacrocolpopexy.

## Potential Risks and Complications

As with any surgery, there are potential risks, including:

- Bleeding or infection
- Injury to surrounding structures
- Pain or discomfort
- Urinary or bowel dysfunction
- Recurrence of prolapse
- Painful intercourse

Your surgeon will discuss these risks in detail during your consultation.

## Is Native Tissue Repair Right for You?

Native tissue repair might be a good option if:

- You have symptomatic pelvic organ prolapse
- Conservative treatments haven't provided sufficient relief
- You prefer using your own tissue rather than synthetic materials
- You're done with childbearing (pregnancy can affect repair results)

Every approach has its own benefits and risks. You and your surgeon should choose together among native tissue repair, a mesh-based repair, and non-surgical options, based on your anatomy, symptoms, and goals.

## Preparing for Your Surgery

If you and your doctor decide on native tissue repair:

1. Complete any pre-operative tests or evaluations
2. Discuss any medications you're taking with your doctor
3. Arrange for help at home during your recovery period
4. Follow all pre-operative instructions provided by your surgical team

Deciding on surgery is personal. Your urogynecologist will go over all your options with you so you can choose what fits your situation.
