# Can you use vaginal estrogen after breast cancer?

Often yes, but this is a shared decision rather than a simple yes or no. For genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM), the vaginal dryness, irritation, and pain with sex that follow the drop in estrogen, nonhormonal treatments come first. If those are not enough, low-dose vaginal estrogen may still be an option after multidisciplinary shared decision-making that includes your oncologist. The AUA/SUFU/AUGS guideline places this as an expert-opinion position, so the answer depends on your situation.

## Why breast cancer raises the question

GSM is more common in breast cancer survivors, affecting more than half of postmenopausal women in this group, partly because cancer treatments can bring these changes on early or make them worse. Yet it is frequently missed and left untreated, often because of reluctance to use estrogen after breast cancer. That reluctance is understandable but often outdated.

Local vaginal estrogen is not the same as systemic hormone therapy. It acts mainly in the vaginal tissue and results in substantially less systemic absorption than the pills or patches taken for whole-body menopause symptoms. Observational studies, including the Women's Health Initiative and a nested case-control study, have not shown an increase in breast cancer recurrence with local estrogen. What is missing is a definitive placebo-controlled trial in breast cancer survivors, so the safety data are reassuring but not conclusive.

That evidence gap is why many clinicians have been cautious, and why GSM in survivors has often gone untreated. At one cancer center, only 39.8% of women with documented GSM symptoms received any treatment. Even so, the guideline says that for a patient with a personal history of breast cancer, a clinician may recommend low-dose vaginal estrogen within multidisciplinary shared decision-making.

## Nonhormonal treatments come first

Vaginal moisturizers, used on a regular schedule, and lubricants, used for sexual activity, are the first-line choice for anyone with a breast cancer history. Pelvic floor physical therapy and vaginal dilators can help as well. Many women get enough relief from these alone. Low-dose vaginal estrogen becomes an option when symptoms persist despite a fair trial of nonhormonal options.

## Tamoxifen versus an aromatase inhibitor

The type of endocrine therapy you take matters, and it is one reason the decision belongs with your oncologist. Tamoxifen acts as an estrogen blocker in breast tissue, so the small, temporary rises in blood hormone levels that can occur with local estrogen are less concerning. Aromatase inhibitors work differently. They lower estradiol to very low, often undetectable levels, which is the whole point of the treatment, so any systemic absorption of estrogen is more of a concern. GSM symptoms also tend to be more severe on an aromatase inhibitor. For some women in this situation, the options discussed with the oncologist include using local hormone therapy after review, or considering a switch to tamoxifen.

## Choosing a lower-absorption formulation

If you and your team decide to try vaginal estrogen, the formulations with the least systemic absorption may be preferred. The 10 microgram estradiol vaginal tablet and the estradiol vaginal ring show the lowest measured systemic levels. Checking blood estrogen levels is not recommended as a way to judge breast cancer risk, because those measurements have not been shown to guide decisions. Using low-dose vaginal estrogen also does not by itself call for endometrial monitoring.

## Coordinate with your oncologist

This is a joint decision. Your oncologist knows your receptor status, your endocrine therapy, and your recurrence risk, and that information shapes whether and how to use local estrogen. If you would rather avoid estrogen entirely, vaginal DHEA is a nonestrogen prescription option. It has not been formally studied in breast cancer survivors, so keep that in mind. Bring your symptoms to both your urogynecologist and your oncologist so the plan reflects your quality of life and your cancer care together.
