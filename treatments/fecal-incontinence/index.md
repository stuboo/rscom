# Fecal incontinence treatments

Fecal incontinence, also called accidental bowel leakage, is the loss of stool or gas when you don't mean to. Most women get meaningful help, and many need nothing more than changes to diet, stool consistency and bowel habits. This page lays out the main options. Each one links to a page with more detail.

## Choosing a treatment

There isn't a single fixed order. The right choice depends on what's causing the leakage, how much it bothers you, what you've already tried and what you're willing to do. Leakage with urgency and loose stool calls for different treatment than leakage you don't feel happening. We go through the options together and pick a plan that fits your goals.

{: .important-title }

> Did you know?
>
> Most women with fecal incontinence have mild symptoms that improve once stool consistency is under control. Open surgery is rarely needed.

## Treatments without surgery

1. Diet and fiber. Firmer, more predictable stool is easier to hold. A fiber supplement such as psyllium reduced leakage episodes compared with placebo in a randomized trial. See [diet changes](/treatments/fecal-incontinence/dietary-adjustments).

2. Medicine. Loperamide (Imodium) firms up loose stool and can cut down on urgency. Constipation is its most common side effect, so we adjust the dose.

3. Bowel retraining. A regular toilet routine helps you empty at predictable times and avoid accidents. See [bowel retraining](/treatments/fecal-incontinence/bowel-retraining).

4. Pelvic floor physical therapy. A pelvic floor therapist works on muscle strength, coordination and rectal sensation, often with biofeedback. In a large trial, biofeedback did about as well as education alone at six months, but many women still find it helps, and it carries little risk. See [physical therapy](/treatments/fecal-incontinence/physical-therapy).

5. Eclipse device. The Eclipse System is a vaginal insert with a small balloon. You inflate it to press on the rectum and deflate it when you need to have a bowel movement. In one study, about three out of four women cut their leakage episodes at least in half by three months. It involves no surgery and no medicine.

Anal bulking injections are another option you may read about. The effect fades over time and often needs repeat injections. I don't offer them.

## Sacral neuromodulation

If these treatments don't give you enough control, sacral neuromodulation is the procedure I recommend most. It's sometimes called a bowel pacemaker. A thin wire sits next to the sacral nerves in your lower back and connects to a small battery placed under the skin of the upper buttock. The mild electrical pulses seem to change the signals between the bowel and the nerves that control it, though the exact mechanism isn't fully understood.

It happens in two stages:
1. A test phase. I place a temporary wire and you wear a small device outside the body, usually for one to three weeks.
2. If your leakage drops by at least half during the test, a permanent device goes in under the skin. The battery lasts about 10 to 15 years.

In the main U.S. study, more than 80% of women cut their weekly leakage episodes at least in half, and the benefit held up at five years. About a third had no leakage at all at five years. A damaged anal sphincter doesn't rule you out, and that includes women whose sphincter repair didn't work. See [sacral neuromodulation](/treatments/fecal-incontinence/sacral-nerve-stimulation).

## Surgery

For most women, open surgery comes up only after other treatments haven't worked:

- Sphincteroplasty: repair of a torn anal sphincter muscle. I rarely recommend it. It often helps at first, but in long-term studies fewer than half of women still had good control after five years. Recovery is painful, and wound infection and other complications are common. Repair right after a delivery injury is a different situation.
- Artificial bowel sphincter: an implanted cuff that keeps the anal canal closed. It is rarely used, has high complication rates, and is not a procedure I perform.
- Colostomy: an opening in the belly so stool passes into a bag. It's a last resort for severe cases, and most women who have one report better quality of life afterward.

{: .important-title }

> Important
> 
> Sacral neuromodulation has largely replaced sphincter repair as the first procedure for fecal incontinence, even when the sphincter is damaged. See [surgical options](/treatments/fecal-incontinence/surgical-options).

## Making your decision

### Questions to ask your doctor

- What is likely causing my fecal incontinence?
- Which treatment option do you recommend for my situation and why?
- What are the potential side effects or complications of each treatment?
- How long might it take to see improvement with each treatment option?
- Are there any lifestyle changes I should make alongside treatment?

## Next steps

At your first visit I'll ask about your bowel habits, stool consistency and what you've already tried, and examine the pelvic floor and anal sphincter. Then we'll pick where to start.
