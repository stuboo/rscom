# Urinary incontinence in female athletes

If you leak during running, jumping, lifting, or other high-impact activities, you're far from alone. About 40% of healthy women who have never given birth leak during vigorous exercise. Half of young women who've never had a baby leak now and then with a cough, a sneeze, or exercise. It affects women of all ages, from high school athletes to recreational runners to competitive CrossFitters. And it doesn't mean you need to give up the activities you love.

## Why this happens

High-impact activities create sudden spikes in pressure inside your belly. Your pelvic floor muscles and the tissue under your urethra work together to keep you dry. Running, jumping, box jumps, double-unders, heavy squats, and gymnastics all push down on the bladder with a force your urethra has to resist. When your pelvic floor support can't keep up with those demands, you leak.

This is a form of [stress urinary incontinence](/conditions/stress-urinary-incontinence). It's common and undertreated. Fewer than 4 in 10 women with stress leaking ever get care for it. Many athletes assume it's normal and work around it with dark shorts, pads, or quiet changes to their training. You don't have to accept it.

Some athletes also get sudden urges and leak on the way to the bathroom. That's urge incontinence, and it's treated differently. If you have both, that's [mixed incontinence](/conditions/mixed-urinary-incontinence), and we start with whichever type bothers you more.

## Signs you should seek help

- Leaking during any type of exercise
- Changing your training to avoid certain movements
- Wearing pads or dark clothing during workouts
- Cutting back on exercise intensity because of leaking

If any of this sounds familiar, come in. These problems are treatable.

## Treatment options

The right approach depends on your symptoms, your sport, and what matters most to you. You don't have to try these in order. Here are the options we can talk through together:

- Sport-specific pelvic floor training: a [pelvic floor therapist](/treatments/urinary-incontinence/pelvic-floor-physical-therapy) who works with athletes can teach you to tighten your pelvic floor during high-demand movements. This includes strength, coordination, timing ("the knack," which means squeezing your pelvic floor just before a jump or lift), relaxation, endurance, and work on your hips, core, and surrounding muscles. In a Cochrane review, women with stress leaking who did pelvic floor muscle training were about 8 times more likely to report cure than women who got no treatment.
- Breathing and bracing: learning to coordinate your breath, core, and pelvic floor during lifts can cut down on leaking.
- Activity changes: short-term, targeted changes while you build strength and coordination. The goal is always to get back to full activity.
- Pessary: a small support device you wear inside the vagina. Incontinence pessaries have a knob that presses gently under the urethra, and many athletes wear one only during workouts. In a large trial, about 4 in 10 women using a pessary felt much better after 3 months, close to the results with pelvic floor training. Not everyone gets a comfortable fit, so it can take a visit or two to find the right size.
- [Urethral bulking](/treatments/urinary-incontinence/urethral-bulking): an office procedure under local numbing where I inject a gel (I use Bulkamid) around the urethra to help it seal. In one long-term study, about two in three women still had improvement or cure 7 years later. It's less likely than a sling to make you completely dry, and it may not hold up as well under heavy impact.
- [Midurethral sling](/treatments/urinary-incontinence/sling-procedures): a brief outpatient surgery that places a thin strip of mesh under the urethra. It's the most effective treatment for stress leaking, with long-term results reported out to 17 years. If you're planning a pregnancy, you can still have a sling. In one study, pregnancy and delivery after a sling didn't raise the chance of leaking coming back, though many women choose to wait until they're done having children.

## How I approach it

I never tell an athlete to stop exercising. We figure out which movements make you leak and match the treatment to your sport and your goals. I have pelvic floor physical therapists in my office, so you can often start therapy in the same place you see me.

Your pelvic floor should be as ready for your sport as the rest of you.

## Your next steps

If leaking is affecting your training or your confidence, schedule a visit. Bring a note of which exercises make you leak and how often it happens.
